The Sea in Front of Masinloc Is Not Empty Blue Space

It is a legally protected area with a proclamation number, a management board and an enforcement problem. Here is what the law actually says about the water people fish in.

Published 2026-08-23

From the Baywalk the water in front of Masinloc looks like any other stretch of the West Philippine Sea: blue, busy in the early morning, empty by the middle of the afternoon. It is not legally ordinary, and it has not been since 1993.

On 18 August 1993, Proclamation No. 231 set aside the coastal area and islands within Masinloc and Oyon Bay as a marine reserve, on the recommendation of the Secretary of Environment and Natural Resources, under the National Integrated Protected Areas System Act of 1992. The stated purpose was to protect the ecological, scenic, scientific and educational features of the area while allowing the social and economic needs of local communities to be met without damaging it. That second clause is the whole difficulty of a protected area with people living in it, written into the founding document.

What it covers now

The reserve has since been reconstituted as the Masinloc and Oyon Bay Protected Landscape and Seascape, usually shortened to MOBPLS. It covers more than seven and a half thousand hectares and reaches across two municipalities, Masinloc and Palauig, and it is the only legislated marine protected area in the region under the Expanded National Integrated Protected Areas System Act of 2018. Legislated matters: it means the area's status rests on a law rather than on an executive instrument that a later administration can quietly revise.

A protected area with people living in it is not a nature reserve. It is a negotiation with a legal deadline attached.

San Salvador Island sits inside this area, and the community-based marine management work done there from the late 1980s onward is among the better documented examples of its kind in the Philippines. That work predates the proclamation. It is one of the cases where a national designation arrived to formalise something local people had already started rather than to impose something new.

What protection does not mean

A designation is not a fence. Enforcement across seven and a half thousand hectares of open water requires boats, fuel, personnel and a reason for a fisherman a long way from shore to believe he will be seen. The Department of Environment and Natural Resources has run enforcement capacity work for the area in recent years, which is a reasonable indication both that the problem is real and that somebody is treating it as real.

The useful thing to understand, if you are reading this from somewhere else, is that the boundary of Masinloc does not stop at the beach. A meaningful part of the municipality's territory, economy and legal identity is underwater.
