Why Does a Distant Shoal Carry Masinloc's Name?

Bajo de Masinloc lies about 124 nautical miles from the Zambales coast. Its old name links a remote reef to this town—but a name on a chart proves less, and more, than political slogans suggest.

Published 2026-08-23 · Updated 2026-08-25

About 124 nautical miles west of Masinloc, a ring of reef encloses a lagoon and only a few rocks remain above water at high tide. It appears in current records as Scarborough Shoal, Panatag Shoal and Bajo de Masinloc. That last name creates the puzzle: why should a feature so far offshore carry the name of this particular town?

Bajo is Spanish for a shoal or shallow. Historical charts show that cartographers knew the feature and related it by name to Masinloc. The surviving name is evidence of a durable geographic association. It does not, without a navigator's log or naming record, tell us who first used the name or exactly why Masinloc rather than another coastal town became its reference point.

What a map can prove

Historical cartography is genuine evidence and should be treated as such. A shoal drawn, named and positioned on official charts across a long period establishes that it was known, that it was known by that name, and that it was understood in relation to a particular coast. Those are facts about the historical record, and they are not small.

What a map cannot do on its own is settle a modern legal question. The 2016 South China Sea arbitration examined historic rights, maritime entitlements and the legal status of features under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. Those are questions an old navigation chart was never drawn to answer. Historical cartography can establish knowledge and naming; modern law supplies a different test.

The old name is real evidence about the past. It is not, by itself, an answer to a present-day legal question — and pretending otherwise weakens rather than strengthens the case.

This distinction is worth holding onto for a reason that has nothing to do with diplomacy. Overstating what a source proves is the fastest way to lose the argument in front of anyone who checks. The strongest version of the historical case is the accurate one.

The local scale of it

At 124 nautical miles, the shoal is farther from the town than a photograph of the bay might make it feel. Yet one of its names still places Masinloc inside every careful account of the dispute. The honest local significance is that tension: geographic distance joined to historical naming, not a claim that the reef sits just beyond the beach.

Still open

This account deliberately stops short of the contemporary legal dispute, which is outside what this project can responsibly document. If you hold reproductions of specific Spanish-era charts showing Bajo de Masinloc with clear dating and provenance, that is exactly the material this article should be built out from.
