Why Is There a Shoal Named After Masinloc?

Bajo de Masinloc appears on Spanish-era charts under that name long before it appeared in anyone's news bulletin. What old maps can settle, and what they cannot.

Published 2026-08-23

About a hundred and twenty-four nautical miles west of Zambales there is a triangle of reef enclosing a lagoon, with a few rocks breaking the surface at high tide. It has at least three names in current use. English-language charts call it Scarborough Shoal, after a British East India Company ship wrecked there in the eighteenth century. Filipino official usage calls it Panatag Shoal. And Spanish colonial cartography called it Bajo de Masinloc — the shoal of Masinloc — which is where this article starts, because that name is the reason a small municipality's name turns up in international law.

The naming is not incidental. A bajo is a shallow, a hazard to shipping, and hazards get named for the nearest place a pilot would recognise from the coast. That the shoal was named for Masinloc rather than for Iba or Santa Cruz tells you something modest but real about which stretch of that coastline mariners were navigating from.

What a map can prove

Historical cartography is genuine evidence and should be treated as such. A shoal drawn, named and positioned on official charts across a long period establishes that it was known, that it was known by that name, and that it was understood in relation to a particular coast. Those are facts about the historical record, and they are not small.

What a map cannot do on its own is settle a modern legal question. Contemporary maritime entitlement is decided under a body of law — principally the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea — that turns on questions old charts were never drawn to answer: baselines, the legal status of individual features, exclusive economic zones, and the conduct of states over time. A chart is admissible material within that argument. It is not a substitute for it.

The old name is real evidence about the past. It is not, by itself, an answer to a present-day legal question — and pretending otherwise weakens rather than strengthens the case.

This distinction is worth holding onto for a reason that has nothing to do with diplomacy. Overstating what a source proves is the fastest way to lose the argument in front of anyone who checks. The strongest version of the historical case is the accurate one.

The local scale of it

For most people in Masinloc the shoal is not an abstraction and not a headline. It is a long way out, it is where certain boats used to go, and its name is their town's name. That is a strange thing to live with: a place you may never see carrying your address into rooms you will never enter.

Still open

This account deliberately stops short of the contemporary legal dispute, which is outside what this project can responsibly document. If you hold reproductions of specific Spanish-era charts showing Bajo de Masinloc with clear dating and provenance, that is exactly the material this article should be built out from.
