Binabayani: The Battle Masinloc Performs Every November

The war dance is publicly documented with the feast of San Andres on 30 November. Its living performance is clear; the often-repeated 1621 origin is not established by a located primary source.

Published 2026-08-23 · Updated 2026-08-25

The Department of Tourism describes Binabayani as Masinloc's depiction of a war between Christians and Aetas, held with the feast of San Andres on 30 November. Project-approved photography from the 2025 performance records costume, movement and staged confrontation. Those are observable facts about a living public event.

The performance joins civic festival, Catholic devotion and a local narrative of conflict. That combination gives Binabayani cultural weight, but it does not grant every printed origin story equal evidence. A tradition can be alive in the present while the date of its first performance remains unknown.

The date problem

Modern accounts very often give 1621 as the year Binabayani began, sometimes attaching an early telling to Capt. Florentino Elicano and a connection to Municipal Judge Gerardo Elicano, and sometimes attaching a narrative about an image or bell recovered from the sea. The research behind this project went looking for the document under the date and could not find one.

So the accurate sentence is not that Binabayani began in 1621. It is that Binabayani is a living Masinloc tradition, commonly interpreted as a conflict between Aetas and Christians, joined to devotion to San Andres and to local stories about something arriving from the sea — and that the 1621 origin, the attribution, the bell narrative and the first performance are not established by any located primary source.

Calling oral tradition oral tradition does not demote it. It protects it from being checked and found wanting against a standard it never claimed.

Why removing the date is not a loss

It feels like one. The instinct is that a tradition with a date is more serious than a tradition without one, and that giving up the date gives up standing. The opposite is closer to the truth. A date that collapses the first time a researcher checks it takes the rest of the account down with it. A tradition described accurately as a living practice, with its origins openly unresolved, is not vulnerable in that way.

There is also a question worth sitting with about what the dance depicts. A performance built around a conflict between Aetas and Christians is a performance about this coast's own history of encounter, and the people on one side of that encounter are not an abstraction. They have descendants. How the tradition is described matters for that reason as well as for the documentary one.
