Heritage

A church built, damaged, and rebuilt: San Andres across the centuries

Heritage buildings are usually described as if survival were passive. A church survives because people keep rebuilding it.

MABAYANI 22 August 2026 6 min read Faith, Conflict & Power · part 5 of 10

Once I stopped looking for a single date, the building started to make sense. San Andres Church has passed through earthquakes, damaged masonry, changing roofs, political upheaval, neglect and modern restoration. Every repair changed it. What stands now is not a frozen eighteenth-century object; it carries decisions from many periods.

The documented phases

The 1825 restoration under Miguel Gregorio Jimenez is among the strongest documented phases: he is described as working extensively on the old church after taking over parish administration. Jose Julian Aranguren's service from 1836 to 1843 belongs to a later strengthening of parish life, though individual architectural details attributed to him should be checked against original sources rather than assumed.

When Fray Agustin Perez returned to Masinloc in December 1901 he reported that the church and convent had been spared the serious destruction suffered by many houses and buildings. The revolution had not destroyed it. Neglect and loss were a different matter.

The twentieth century

A strong earthquake damaged the church in 1970, and a repair phase followed in 1971. Research files connect that phase with major facade work and with the modern right-side belfry and cupola, under Columban Father Donal O'Dea. That attribution matters for the visual history of the building, and it is one of the items still awaiting confirmation from parish records and dated photographs.

Where the evidence stands

Documented core, with the 1971 facade attribution still a research lead rather than a settled fact.

The December 1999 earthquake damaged the facade again. The church was restored and declared a National Cultural Treasure on 31 July 2001. In 2021 the parish was publicly elevated as the Diocesan Shrine of San Andres.

The line from 1607 to the present is not one uninterrupted building. It is an uninterrupted relationship between a community and a sacred place.

Why visual accuracy is historical accuracy

This is where I became careful about pictures. A convincing image can still be wrong. A scene set in 1607 needs a light chapel. A nineteenth or early twentieth-century scene needs the older facade. Only the present can show the modern tower and cupola. Each image should declare its period, because a modern feature does not become ancient by being attached to an old church.

That makes the building useful in a classroom. Students can compare photographs, work out which elements appeared later, and learn that conservation is not simply keeping an object old — it is understanding which parts belong to which time, and deciding how to preserve the whole sequence.

Masinloqueños at home and abroad contributed to restoration. Parish communities protected records. Clergy documented some changes and failed to document others. Earthquakes exposed weak points. The strongest thing about San Andres is not that it never changed. It is that the community continued through the changes.

Still open

A National Cultural Treasure marker is a separate event from the declaration, and the two are easy to merge into one date. A marker unveiling in November 2012 has been mentioned to us but no National Museum, Philippine Information Agency, Philippine News Agency or Official Gazette record for it has been located. Until one is, this account carries the declaration date only. If you hold a photograph of the marker, a programme, or a parish or municipal record of the unveiling, that is the missing piece.
