Why MABAYANI exists

Eight stories, one argument, and the last thing left to say — including the part I have been keeping back.

By Francine Marie Bautista 22 August 2026 6 min read Closing · part 10 of 10

I started this the way most people start: by trying to confirm something I already believed. I wanted the founding date. I wanted the year that would let me say Masinloc is this old, here is the proof, put it on the seal.

I did not find it. What I found instead was a date being repeated because it had been repeated, a church that turned out to be several churches, a battle whose heroes are anonymous in the only account that records them, and a language whose name is an argument.

Every one of those was a loss before it was a finding. I want to be honest about that, because the pressure to write the other version never really goes away.

What the work kept asking me to do

There is a version of this project that would have performed much better. It fills the gap before 1607 with named chiefs. It gives Binabayani a clean origin year. It lets the 1798 earthquake stand because the story is tidier with it. It says 1572 and moves on, because that is what people expect to read and nobody checks.

I understand the appeal. An invented date makes a festival look older. An invented chief makes a chapter feel complete. A dramatic scene makes people share the work. Each of those choices is available at every stage, and each one is easier than the alternative.

The brave choice is sometimes to leave a space empty and write: we have not verified this yet.

So the rule I set was narrow and unglamorous. Say what the evidence carries. Say plainly where it stops. Label oral tradition as oral tradition and treat that as a description, not a demotion. Never upgrade an uncertain claim because the uncertain version reads badly.

What this piece is

Reflection, not reporting. The historical claims it refers back to are documented in the eight articles before it; this one explains the method, not new evidence.

Where the name comes from

MABAYANI is a chosen name and an interpretive frame. It is not offered as a dictionary entry, and nothing here should be read as a claim about the meaning of a Sambal word.

I chose it because bravery is the thread that kept reappearing in the material, and because the kind of bravery I kept finding was not the kind that ends up on a banner. In 1649 many residents ran for the mountains and others organised a defence, and both of those were survival. A chapel made of light materials disappeared and a stone church went up in its place and earthquakes damaged that and people rebuilt it, across generations, without any of them being named in what survives.

Binabayani is danced every year, and the courage in it is not only the staged fight. It takes something to say out loud that 1621 is not yet proven. It takes something to look again at a costume that has been familiar for decades. It takes something to notice that the Christian faction in the dance is not the same as Spanish authority, and that flattening the two changes what the whole performance is saying.

A language goes quiet in the same undramatic way. One child answers in Tagalog. One family stops using one word. One spelling becomes uncertain and nobody is sure enough to correct it. Keeping Sambal means speaking it imperfectly in public and accepting correction, which is its own small act of nerve.

The sentence this is all built around

If a reader takes one line away from these stories, I would like it to be this: our history is already powerful. We do not need to invent it.

That sentence is doing specific work. It protects pride from turning into insecurity. A community that trusts what actually happened to it does not need false proof of greatness, and does not lose anything when a bad date is withdrawn. Masinloc is not younger because 1572 will not hold. It is not smaller because the defenders of 1649 are unnamed. Those are gaps in the paperwork, not gaps in the town.

What I hope this becomes is not a finished account. It is a better argument. A classroom where students read the 1649 story and immediately ask who the defenders were. A festival workshop that separates local Christian lowlanders from colonial authority. Elders explaining Tina in their own words while a linguist lays out the history of the label beside them. Parish photographs in date order, so that nobody puts the modern tower into a seventeenth-century scene again.

It should not finish the conversation. It should make the conversation better.

The part I have been keeping back

You will have noticed that none of the previous eight stories carried a name. That was deliberate, and this is the point where it ends.

I kept the byline off because I did not want the research read as one person's opinion about Masinloc while it was still being tested. A date either holds or it does not, regardless of who is checking it. I wanted the evidence to be argued with on its own terms first.

MABAYANI was created and written by Francine Marie Bautista, as part of the Mabayani Project for Masinloc, Zambales. Everything published here has been read against the sources listed in the evidence directory, and everything still unresolved has been left marked as unresolved.

It is my reflection. I would like it to become our responsibility. If you can correct something on this site, correct it — a documented objection is worth more to this work than agreement is.

Still open

The names of the 1649 defenders, the earliest surviving reference to Binabayani, a community-approved account of the language name, dated photographs of the church facade before 1970, and a certified roster of municipal leadership all remain unrecovered.
