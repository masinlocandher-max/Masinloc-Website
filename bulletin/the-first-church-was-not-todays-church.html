Heritage

The first church was not the church we see today

One parish, several buildings, and a fifty-eight-year hole in the records where the answer should be.

MABAYANI 22 August 2026 6 min read Faith, Conflict & Power · part 4 of 10

San Andres Church is the strongest single image of Masinloc, and I had been treating it the way most people do: as one object with one birthday. The evidence describes something more layered.

The first mission church of 1607 was built from light materials and did not last. The present stone church belongs to a later period. An 1891 Recollect account by Vicente Pascual, quoted in the modern historical literature, says construction of the present church began in 1745. Pascual drew partly on an older book of notable events and partly on oral tradition collected from elders.

Why the sentence needs two halves

The responsible statement is: construction of the present stone church is traditionally dated to 1745 in a late nineteenth-century account, and the completion date is unknown. Both halves are necessary. Dropping the second one turns a tradition into a certificate.

Where the evidence stands

Documented core, with caveats. The 1745 start is supported by an 1891 account that itself relied partly on oral tradition. Completion is unknown.

The fifty-eight years that are missing

The gap is where I stopped being able to answer anything at all. A baptismal book covering 1726 to 1784 was no longer available, leaving a fifty-eight-year documentary silence across exactly the period when the stone church was going up. Termites, insects, weather, earthquakes, poor paper and ordinary age could all have contributed.

Because of that loss we cannot confidently name the builder, the finishing date, or every construction phase. That is a real limit, and it is worth stating rather than filling.

The 1798 story does not hold

A later oral account claimed the church was completed and then destroyed by an earthquake in 1798. Pascual doubted it and looked. He found no earthquake in 1798. Earthquakes were documented in 1754 and in 1766, and he could not determine which had damaged the building.

It would be easy to publish the clean version: started 1745, completed 1798, destroyed the same year. I wanted to. The clean version is dramatic and it is wrong. The careful version is that construction began in 1745 according to a tradition recorded in 1891, completion is unknown, the 1798 claim is unreliable, and damage may relate to 1754 or 1766 without the exact event being fixable.

Between the first chapel and the present church is not a blank. It is a history of building, damage, missing paper, labour, faith, and repair.

A firmer point to stand on

Miguel Gregorio Jimenez gives a steadier anchor. He took over parish administration in June 1825 and is described as the administrator who worked most substantially on restoring the old church. Jose Julian Aranguren served in Masinloc from 1836 to 1843, before his appointment to the metropolitan see of Manila in 1845.

The church is best understood as a layered structure rather than the work of one friar or one date. It carries builders whose names are missing, local labourers, parish administrators, donors, worshippers, and later generations who repaired what earthquakes and time damaged.

Still open

The 1616 phase, the late nineteenth-century roof and bell-tower changes, and the twentieth-century facade work still need confirmation against the original Recollect archive, parish records, National Museum files and dated photographs.
