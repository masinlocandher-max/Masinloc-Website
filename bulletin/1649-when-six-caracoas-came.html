1649: when six caracoas came to Masinloc

The sea that fed Masinloc also carried danger. What the record preserves, and the names it did not.

MABAYANI 22 August 2026 7 min read Faith, Conflict & Power · part 6 of 10

The most vivid thing I found anywhere in the record happens in 1649. According to the Recollect history, six caracoas landed on the shore of Masinloc. The account identifies the raiding forces as Moros from Jolo and Borneo, and says more than six hundred fighters began to disembark.

The number is hard to picture. Masinloc had no standing police force, no paved roads, no communication system and no immediate military support. Six war vessels and hundreds of armed men would have turned a familiar shoreline into something else entirely.

What the account describes

A great majority of residents fled toward the neighbouring mountain fastness. That is not cowardice; moving families away from a landing force is a rational decision, and the geography of this coast makes it an obvious one. The shore was exposed. The mountains offered refuge.

The record then names Fray Francisco de San Jose, who led the Zambaleños in the defence of their town. The defenders fought, repelled the landing force, and drove the survivors to withdraw to an adjacent islet called Pulo San Salvador. The next day Francisco led a large number of emboldened Zambaleños in attacking the hideout. The account ends with the capture of the caracoas and war material.

Where the evidence stands

Documented in a Recollect historical account. The event is strongly traceable; the wording and viewpoint of the source require critical reading.

Who is named, and who is not

This is the strongest documented episode of collective bravery in the Masinloc story, and it is also where the record failed me most completely. It is written through a Recollect lens. The friar is named. The local fighters are praised as Zambaleños, but not one of them is named individually.

The questions I most wanted to answer are the ones the source answers least. How many of the defenders were local Sambal Christians? Were there people from nearby settlements? What did they fight with? Who carried information? Who protected the elderly and the children? Were women involved in supplies, warning and care? The account does not say.

The names of the Masinloc defenders are a research gap. Their absence should not be hidden behind a single heroic portrait of the friar.

Honouring defenders without producing prejudice

The raiding forces belonged to a seventeenth-century world of maritime warfare, slave raiding, colonial expansion, sultanates, local rivalries and religious conflict. The colonial label Moro was applied broadly to Muslim peoples, and it cannot be used as though every Muslim community across time were a single enemy.

The bravery of Masinloc and the bravery of Muslim communities who resisted Spanish conquest belong in the same national history. One local victory does not make one people superior. It tells us that coastal communities defended themselves in a violent period.

The word pirate needs the same care. Colonial and church accounts used it for maritime raiders who attacked settlements, captured people and seized goods. Written now, the accurate phrasing is the one the source itself supports: raiding forces identified as Moros from Jolo and Borneo.

Told for a school reader, the event works as a sequence rather than a spectacle: arrival, alarm, movement to safety, organisation, defence, withdrawal, pursuit, capture of vessels. The combat formation, shoreline positions, weapons, clothing and casualties are not in the record and should not be invented to fill it.

Still open

Recollect chronicles, parish papers, Spanish military reports and family traditions may yet recover the names of the defenders.
