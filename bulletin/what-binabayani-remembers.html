Oral History

What Binabayani remembers, and what the record can confirm

A ritual does not need a false date to be old. Calling oral tradition by its name is how you protect it.

MABAYANI 22 August 2026 6 min read What Masinloc Remembers · part 7 of 10

Binabayani is not a small part of Masinloc's identity. It carries movement, sound, combat, devotion, costume, fear, courage and public memory. It is performed in connection with the feast of San Andres, and it has been described as a war dance between Aetas and Christians. Around it sits a story of an image, a bell, the shore, and a dance that made the sacred object light enough to move.

The tradition matters. The problem starts when every part of it is printed as though it were a verified seventeenth-century record.

The 1621 problem

Many modern accounts repeat the year 1621, and I could not find the document underneath it. Some attribute an early telling to Capt. Florentino Elicano and connect it to Municipal Judge Gerardo Elicano. The research behind this project flags the date as a likely chronological error, possibly connected to a 1921 reference rather than 1621. No authenticated seventeenth-century manuscript proving the date, the first teller or the exact sequence has been located.

So the honest sentence is not "Binabayani began in 1621." It is that Binabayani is a living Masinloc tradition, commonly interpreted as a conflict between Aetas and Christians, joined to devotion to San Andres and to local stories about an image or bell from the sea — and that its exact origin date and earliest written account are still being researched.

Where the evidence stands

Living tradition and oral memory. The performance is real and continuing. The 1621 origin, the Elicano attribution, the bell narrative and the first performance are not established in any located primary source.

Why this does not weaken the festival

Some readers will feel that removing the date takes something away. I felt it too. The opposite is closer to the truth. When oral tradition is called oral tradition, it is protected — valued for what it actually does in the life of the community: carrying identity, teaching memory, dramatising conflict, expressing devotion, and gathering generations into one public act.

A ritual does not need a false date to be old. A story does not need to become an archive document to be meaningful.

The conflict itself

This is the part I have rewritten most often. Missionary records describe violence between new Christian settlements and upland Aeta groups across parts of Zambales and Pangasinan. Those records show conflict existed, but they come from the missionary side and often use prejudiced language.

Binabayani may preserve a cultural memory of lowland–upland conflict and eventual reconciliation. The current evidence does not prove a single Aeta–Christian war in Masinloc in 1621. There is also a distinction that gets flattened constantly: the Christian side in this memory is not simply the Spanish. Local Christian lowlanders are not the same as colonial actors, and collapsing them changes what the performance is about.

That changes how the dance is best taught. Not as an exact battle on an exact date with named leaders and fixed costumes, but as a living performance through which Masinloc remembers conflict, bravery, faith and reconciliation — a performance with its own history, which has changed as generations changed the choreography, the costume and the explanation.

The belief that calamity may follow if the dance is not performed belongs in the same category: recorded as belief, valuable for what it shows about how seriously earlier generations took the ritual, not presented as a demonstrated cause of events.

Still open

The earliest surviving programme, newspaper report, parish reference, Elicano document, photograph and oral testimony have yet to be traced, along with how the costume and explanation changed across the twentieth century.
